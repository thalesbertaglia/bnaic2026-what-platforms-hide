What Platforms Hide

How can independent researchers study platforms they cannot fully inspect?

Apps & tracking The App Economy: Tracking, Platform Power, and Control

Konrad Kollnig

About the talk

My new book The App Economy (Bristol University Press, 2026, open access) asks how Apple and Google use their dominance over the app economy for their bottom line, with what consequences for society, and how to respond. In this talk, I zoom in on tracking and platform power. Drawing on the book, my TrackerControl app and ongoing research, I explore who controls our apps and data – and how technical tools, law and independent research can help us challenge that control.

Political monetisation How do creators make money from political posts?

Catalina Goanta

About the talk

Political creators may be paid to promote candidates or earn money through livestream gifts, yet these arrangements can be difficult to detect. The proposed Election Monetisation Observatory (EMO) would bring together open-source tools to study hidden advertising in election content, drawing on the expertise of researchers, creators and journalists. This talk considers what those tools could reveal, and where limited platform data and changing features still get in the way.

AI shopping advice Auditing AI-Generated Product Recommendations

Lucas Uberti-Bona Marin

About the talk

Consumers increasingly use AI chatbots for advice on what to buy. With companies like OpenAI and Google monetising their AI through advertising, this raises difficult questions about the bias and impartiality of such advice. In response, we conduct an AI audit of popular chatbots using real commercial-advice queries.

AI labels Auditing Platform's AI-Labelling and AI Detection Practices

Bram Rijsbosch

About the talk

AI labels are emerging as a primary safeguard for transparency about AI-generated content on social media, including under the EU DSA and AI Act. Yet, limited systematic evidence exists on how platforms implement such labels in practice. In response, we conducted a large-scale legally grounded audit of AI labelling practices across several major social media platforms. In this talk, we will share insights on the different methods used in this study, and the types of signals that are currently (in)visible to external auditors.

Read the paper

Why this matters

App stores set the terms for mobile apps. Social media platforms influence which political posts people see. Chatbots offer shopping advice.

These platforms shape everyday choices and public debate, but control much of the data needed to examine them. Researchers can measure app traffic, inspect public posts and audit chatbot answers, yet each gives only a partial view.

This is platform observability in practice: what can we see and test from the outside, and what can those observations actually establish? The talks bring technical, legal and social perspectives to that question, including where current methods fall short and what might make the next study possible.

Group exercise

What should we study next?

Bring a question from your work, or start with one from the talks. In small groups, we will ask what we can observe, what better access would change, and how methods from different fields might help.

01 What deserves a closer look?

An app sharing data, a monetised political video, an unlabelled AI post or a chatbot's product suggestion.

02 What can we observe now?

App traffic, public posts, AI labels or chatbot answers: which would help answer your question?

03 What can other fields add?

AI and NLP, network analysis, law and social research each bring different tools and questions.

04 What gets in the way, and what could help?

Access, privacy, weak labels and changing platforms. Could audits, shared datasets or collaborations help?

Takeaway

Questions worth testing, possible methods and a clearer sense of the evidence still missing.
